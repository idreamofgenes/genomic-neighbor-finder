STEP 02Day 0No filming needed

The real blocker, named properly

You wrote that the biggest problem is having family, friends and ex-colleagues on the account, and that it would be so much easier if it were only strangers. That is the correct diagnosis and it has a name: context collapse — when one post has to work for every audience you have ever had at once, so you write for the harshest reader in the room and end up posting nothing (Marwick & boyd, 2011).

Three straight answers about it.

1. There is no privacy setting that fixes this

On a public account you cannot hide feed posts from specific followers. Close Friends only covers Stories. Removing a follower does not stop them looking — the profile is still public to them. So there is no button. Stop looking for one.

2. The fear is attached to disclosure, not to science

Look at what you are actually afraid of them seeing: the unfinished PhD, the burnout, the years that didn't end where you planned. That is confessional content. But you already decided — in your own words — that you don't want that on iDreamofGenes and don't want to lead with your background right now. You have already solved this and not noticed.

Nobody's aunt has an opinion about how you cut a 5 µm cryosection. Your ex-supervisor has no angle on a BioRender tutorial. Procedural content is low-disclosure by construction: there is nothing in it for anyone to judge, because it is about the work, not about you. The format that performs best for you is the same format that makes your audience problem go away.

3. If you still want a clean break, take it deliberately, not by drifting

Starting fresh is legitimate. But price it honestly: you would give up 725 people, a working series, and three posts that already proved reach — to solve a discomfort that the right format largely removes anyway. My recommendation is to keep the account and change the content. If after eight weeks of posting you find you are still self-censoring, then start clean, with evidence rather than dread.

The one thing to accept

Some people who know you will watch you restart something publicly, and a few will have thoughts. That is the actual cost, and it is unavoidable on any public account. It is also much smaller than it feels at 22:13 on a Tuesday. The people most likely to comment are the ones least likely to be watching in six weeks.

STEP 04Week 1Structural

The anchor series and five pillars

The one thing worth stealing from Yannick's document is structural, not tactical: a counted series with an open end. "Course 7 of 96" converts a viewer into a follower, because there is something to come back for. A standalone good video does not.

The anchor: one sample, start to finish

"From the operating room to the paper — what happens to a tumour sample"

One numbered episode per step. Episode 1 is the sample arriving; the last is the figure in a published paper. Ten to fifteen episodes, then you run it again with a different sample type and different techniques.

Why this and not "Histology 101" on its own: histology is one chapter of a story you can already tell end to end, and you have the footage for most of it — microtome, water bath, H&E, slidescanner, confocal. The journey frame gives every episode a reason to exist and gives the viewer a reason to stay. Histology 101 becomes episodes 3 to 6, not the whole channel.

The five pillars

Every post goes in exactly one. If an idea fits none of them, you don't make it.

35%

The sample's journey

The anchor series. Numbered, same structure each time, one step per episode. This is what people follow you for.

25%

Tools & skills

BioRender, Fiji/ImageJ, figure design, reading a paper, poster layout, reference managers. Your proven top performer and your most-saved content. Saves are the strongest growth signal there is.

20%

What we actually know about cancer

One mechanism per post, in plain language, no jargon left unexplained. This is your bridge to people outside science — and the pillar with the largest possible audience.

15%

The reality of research

The failed experiment, the seven-times-repeated protocol, what a week at the bench actually contains. Builds the attachment that technique posts alone never will — without becoming confessional.

5%

You

Small dose. Switzerland, the lab, your face on camera. Enough to be a person, not enough to be the subject. Zero burnout or PhD-exit content on this account — that belongs to FromHereIGrow or to nobody.

The trap your grid shows

Your family and landscape posts will keep getting warm likes, because 725 people know you personally. That is not a signal — that is your address book. From now on judge every post on non-follower reach, not likes. Your 4'506-view BioRender post probably got fewer likes than the Braunwald hike and was worth ten times as much.

STEP 05Week 1–4The craft

Format, hooks, 24 shootable ideas

The 60-second template — same every time

Recognisability beats variety. The sameness is what turns a format into a channel.

  1. 0–2 s · The object. Hands, the instrument, the tissue. No greeting, no face, no "hi guys". A number or a physical fact on screen.
  2. 2–8 s · The stake. One sentence saying why this step exists and what goes wrong without it.
  3. 8–40 s · The doing. Your hands, close, real time or lightly sped. This is the whole product — people watch for the craft.
  4. 40–52 s · The result. The slide, the screen, the image. The payoff has to be visible.
  5. 52–60 s · The counter and the question. "Step 4 of 12. What do you want to see next?" Never "follow me".

Hooks: the first 1.5 seconds decide everything

✕

"Hi guys, today I'll show you how we do histology."

✓

"This is a piece of a human tumour. It is five micrometres thick — about a tenth of a hair."

✕

"Some tips for making a scientific poster."

✓

"My first conference poster took two days in PowerPoint. This one took forty minutes."

✕

"Let's talk a bit about melanoma."

✓

"Melanoma is one of the most heavily mutated cancers there is. That is exactly why immunotherapy works on it."

✕

"A day in the life of a PhD student."

✓

"I ran this experiment seven times before it worked. Here is what was wrong the first six."

Three rules that always hold. Burn in the subtitles — most people watch without sound, and your accent and pace are not the issue, the silence is. English, not German — your subject is not geographically bound the way Yannick's is, and English multiplies your addressable audience for the same effort. End on a question, every time.

STEP 06Before you filmBlocking

Production, and what to clear before you film

Clear this first — it is not optional

Filming in a research lab that handles human tumour material is not the same as filming a golf course. Before a single reel: talk to your PI and the institute's communications office, and get the answer in writing. Assume, until told otherwise, that unpublished data, anything patient-derived or potentially identifiable, and colleagues' faces and screens are all off-limits. Swiss human-research rules and institutional policy both bite here, and a research institution's reputational sensitivity is real.

This is your version of Yannick's employer conversation, and it is more consequential than his. Do it in week one, not after your first post travels.

One thing I need from you

Do you still have lab access? It changes about a third of this plan. If yes, the anchor series runs as written. If no, it still works — pillars 2, 3 and 4 need no bench at all, your existing archive footage covers several episodes, and "the journey of a sample" can be told with your own past material plus screen recordings and drawn explainers. Don't let this question stall the other six steps.

One lab day = three weeks of content

You have a small child and a full life. A plan needing three shoot days a week dies in week two. Shoot the whole list below in a single session and cut it later:

  • The room, wide, three seconds — your establishing shot
  • The instrument doing its thing, close, on a small tripod, no hands in frame
  • Your hands doing the step, close. Hands outperform faces for craft content, and they cost you nothing in exposure.
  • The one visually striking moment: the section floating on the water bath, the stain going purple, the confocal image resolving on screen
  • A talking-head hook, eight seconds, said at the bench
  • The result — the slide, the screen, the figure
  • Three or four unedited Story fragments as you go

Kit

A phone clamp tripod (~CHF 40) and a clip-on wireless mic (~CHF 100–150). Lab rooms are loud — ventilation and fume hoods are why science reels sound bad. CapCut to edit, free. That is the entire list; you do not need a camera.

Cadence

Two reels a week — Tuesday and Sunday evening. One carousel a fortnight. Stories three days a week. Ninety minutes on Sunday to cut and schedule the week. Two a week sustained for a year beats four a week for a month, and the second is what you'll be tempted to do in October.

STEP 07OngoingMeasurement

Restart, cadence, and what to measure

Do not post an explanation

You have not posted since 2024 and will feel you owe the account a "so, I've been away" post. You don't, and that post would drag you straight into the disclosure you decided against in Step 02. Post 1 is episode 1 of the series. No apology, no preamble. Nobody scrolling past has any idea there was a gap, and the people who do know will work it out.

YouTube — the short answer is no, with one exception

Don't launch a YouTube channel. Long-form is a different craft with a different weekly cost, and starting a second thing before the first is running is how both end up dead. Do create the channel and post the identical vertical videos as Shorts — it costs you the upload and nothing else, it reaches a different audience, and unlike Instagram, YouTube is searchable, so an episode about cryosectioning keeps finding people three years from now. Revisit long-form when iDreamofGenes has been running weekly for six months.

Honest expectation setting

Your niche is smaller per post than golf travel. It is also higher-intent: someone who watches a nine-minute-equivalent explanation of antibody staining is a far more valuable follower than someone who liked a picture of Portugal. I'm not going to give you a follower number and a date, because I'd be making it up. What I will say is that the constraint on this account is not audience size — cancer is one of the most-searched health subjects there is — it is whether you post twice a week for six months. Weeks three to eight will feel flat. That is the normal shape of the curve and it is where most people stop.
